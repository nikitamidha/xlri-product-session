# Jobs to be Done in the AI-Native World

Two parts, in this order:

**Part 1 — Forming the job.** The framework itself, the four forces defined, and
then the jobs actually formed for our portfolio.

**Part 2 — What changed, before and after generative AI.** The delta, read
through the same four forces.

The order matters. You cannot say what changed until the room can state the job
and score the forces. Part 1 is the instrument; Part 2 is the reading.

# PART 1 — Forming the job

## 1.1 What a job is, and what it isn't

A **job to be done** is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular
circumstance. It is not a task, not an activity, not a feature, and never a
product category.

| Not a job | Why not | The actual job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Dictating" | An activity | Getting a thought out of my head |
| "Using Wispr Flow" | A solution | — |
| "Voice input" | A category | — |
| "Being more productive" | Too abstract to be falsifiable | — |

**The test:** strike every product and technology name from your job statement.
If what remains still reads as true, and as *old* — twenty years old, ideally
older — you have a job. If it stops making sense, you had a feature.

## 1.2 The job statement

> **When** *[circumstance]*, **I want to** *[motivation]*, **so I can**
> *[expected outcome]*.

Three elements, and you need all three. The circumstance is what makes the job
arise; the motivation is the job proper; the expected outcome is the standard by
which the person judges whether they got what they came for.

Hold onto that third element. In Part 2 it turns out to be the one that moved.

## 1.3 Every job has three layers

Most product failures are failures at a layer nobody wrote down.

| Layer | The question | Wispr example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Functional** | What is the practical task? | Convert a thought into usable text |
| **Emotional** | How do I want to feel — or avoid feeling? | Not experience myself as the bottleneck to my own thinking |
| **Social** | How do I want to be perceived? | **Produce text that reads as competent — nobody should be able to tell I dictated it** |

Forty years of dictation products served the functional layer and ignored the
social one. That omission is most of the story.

## 1.4 The four forces — what they are

The forces of progress. This is the part to put on a slide and leave up.

A person only switches from what they do now to something new when four forces
resolve in favour of the change. **Two push toward change. Two push back.**

---

### Force 1 — PUSH of the situation

**What it is:** the frustration with how things are now. It comes from the
person's circumstance, not from any product. Push is what makes someone start
looking in the first place.

**The question to ask:** *What is going badly enough that they'd consider
changing anything at all?*

**What it sounds like:** "I can't keep doing this." "This is killing me."

**Note:** you cannot manufacture Push. It belongs to the user's situation. You
can only find people who already have it.

---

### Force 2 — PULL of the new solution

**What it is:** the attraction of a specific new thing — a vivid picture of life
with it. Pull is about the destination.

**The question to ask:** *What does the new thing promise that they can actually
imagine?*

**What it sounds like:** "If this worked, I could finally…"

**Note:** almost all marketing and most roadmaps operate here. Which is exactly
why marketing alone so rarely moves entrenched behaviour — you are pushing on
one of four forces, and usually not the binding one.

---

### Force 3 — ANXIETY of the new solution

**What it is:** everything uncertain about the new thing. It is the most
under-managed force in product work, and it splits usefully in two:

| | | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anxiety in choice** | *Before* adopting | Will it work for me? How long to learn? What does it cost me to try and fail? |
| **Anxiety in use** | *After* adopting | Will it let me down at a bad moment? Will I be able to tell when it's wrong? Will I look foolish? |

**The question to ask:** *What could go wrong — in their imagination, not in
reality?*

**What it sounds like:** "Yeah, but what if…"

**Note this now, because Part 2 turns on it:** AI products carry unusually high
**anxiety in use**, because the output is probabilistic and the user often can't
verify it. That is precisely what citations, diffs, previews and undo exist to
address. Those are not features. They are anxiety instruments.

---

### Force 4 — HABIT of the present

**What it is:** attachment to the current way — not because it is good, but
because it is automatic and already paid for. Muscle memory, sunk investment,
and often identity.

**The question to ask:** *What have they already invested that switching would
write off?*

**What it sounds like:** "It's fine. I'm used to it."

---

### The equation

> **A switch happens only when PUSH + PULL > ANXIETY + HABIT.**

**And here is the practical consequence most teams miss.** Of the four:

- **Push** you cannot create — it's the user's circumstance.
- **Pull** has sharply diminishing returns against an entrenched habit. Being
  30% better than something automatic and free buys you nothing.
- **Anxiety** and **Habit** are the two levers actually available to a product
  team.

**Almost nobody works on those two.** Hold that thought.

## 1.5 Outcome statements

A job statement without outcome statements is a slogan. Outcomes are how you
make the job measurable, in one of two forms:

> **Minimize the time it takes to** *[step]*
> **Minimize the likelihood that** *[undesirable state]*

The useful move is to write out every outcome for a job and then mark each one
**served / poorly served / unserved** by the current solutions. The unserved
rows are the opportunity — and occasionally you find a row the incumbent makes
*actively worse*, which is where the largest openings hide.

## 1.6 The competitive set includes non-consumption

The biggest competitor for most jobs is **not doing the thing at all**. It never
appears on a market map, and in AI-native markets it is usually the segment
being converted.

# Forming the jobs

## 1.7 Wispr Flow, worked in full

### The job

> **When** I have a formed thought and an empty text field in front of me,
> **I want to** get it out of my head at something near the speed I think it,
> **so I can** stay in the flow of the actual work instead of becoming my own
> transcriptionist.

Strike "Wispr Flow" and the statement still holds — and would have held in 1985.
That's the test passing.

| Layer | The job |
| --- | --- |
| Functional | Convert an internal thought into external, usable text |
| Emotional | Not be the bottleneck to my own thinking |
| Social | **Produce text nobody can tell was dictated** |

### The outcomes, and who served them

| Outcome statement | Pre-gen-AI |
| --- | --- |
| Minimize the time to get a formed thought into a text field | Partly served |
| **Minimize the effort to structure a thought before expressing it** | **Made actively worse** |
| Minimize the likelihood the output needs editing before it can be sent | Unserved |
| **Minimize the likelihood the recipient can tell it was dictated** | **Unserved** |
| Minimize the time to correct a misrecognised word | Poorly served |
| Minimize the effort to adapt the same content to a different destination | Unserved |
| Minimize the setup effort before first useful output | Unserved |

**Row two is the whole case.** Old dictation didn't merely fail that outcome — it
made it worse than typing, by demanding you compose finished prose in your head
and then perform it aloud. That is precisely the cognitive labour the job exists
to avoid.

### The competitive set

| Competitor | Share of the job |
| --- | --- |
| Typing | Dominant |
| **Not writing it down at all** | **Large, and on no market map** |
| A human assistant | Small — priced out of reach |
| Dictation software | **Near zero, after four decades of free distribution** |

### The four forces, scored — pre-gen-AI

| Force | Strength | What was in the user's head |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Push** | *Moderate* | I think faster than I type; my hands hurt. **But note what I was typing: email, documents, chat — text aimed at other humans. Bounded by how much I had to communicate.** |
| **Pull** | *Moderate* | Speech is ~3× faster. True — but theoretical, and I've heard it before. |
| **Anxiety — in choice** | **Very strong** | Voice enrollment. A custom dictionary. Do I have to *train* it? |
| **Anxiety — in use** | **Very strong** | If the output is garbage I've now lost the time twice. And I'd be talking at my laptop in an open-plan office. |
| **Habit** | **Very strong** | Twenty-five years of typing. It costs me no conscious thought at all. |

**Push + Pull < Anxiety + Habit → no switch. For forty years.**

Hold the Push row lightly. In Part 2 it turns out to be the force that moved
most — and the one that moved *first*.

That inequality is the most valuable thing on the slide: a genuine job, real
demand, a working solution, free distribution — and no hires, because the forces
never resolved.

## 1.8 The portfolio, jobs formed

Each as a job story, plus **the force that was actually binding.** That second
column is what Part 2 acts on.

| Product | The job | Binding force |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wispr Flow** | When I have a formed thought and an empty text field, I want to get it out at the speed I think, so I can stay in the work rather than transcribing myself. | **Anxiety** (both kinds) |
| **Descript** | When I have raw footage and a point to make, I want to shape it without learning a timeline editor, so I can publish while the idea is still relevant. | **Anxiety in choice** — a new skill |
| **Granola** | When I must both participate in a meeting and remember it, I want a record of what *I* judged important, so I can act on it without re-listening. | **Anxiety in use** — a bot in the room; notes that aren't mine |
| **Cursor** | When I know what the code should do but not the exact syntax, I want to describe the change and see it, so I can spend attention on judgement. | **Anxiety in use** — will it break my codebase? |
| **NotebookLM** | When I'm accountable for material I haven't time to read, I want to interrogate it as if I had, so I can make a decision I can defend. | **Anxiety in use** — can I trust this? |
| **Khanmigo** | When I'm stuck and there's nobody to ask, I want someone patient to get me unstuck, so I can keep going without being handed the answer. | **Habit + social anxiety** — asking costs face |
| **Duolingo practice** | When I want to practise speaking and I'm embarrassed to be bad in front of a person, I want a partner who won't judge me, so I can build fluency without the social cost. | **Anxiety** — purely emotional |
| **Lovable** | When I have an idea for a tool and no engineer, I want to see it working, so I can find out whether I actually want it. | **Anxiety in choice** + weak Pull |
| **Midjourney** | When there's an image in my head I can't draw or describe, I want to iterate toward it, so I can get something usable without commissioning anyone. | **Weak Pull** — couldn't picture the output |
| **Google Lens** | When I'm looking at something I can't name, I want to search for it directly, so I can find out what it is without guessing at words. | **No candidate existed** |
| **Gamma** | When I have an argument to make and a deadline, I want a structure to react to, so I can spend time on the thinking rather than the formatting. | **Habit** — the deck template |
| **Perplexity** | When I need a defensible answer fast, I want the answer and its sources together, so I can use it without doing the research twice. | **Anxiety in use** |

### Two patterns, and one is far more interesting

Reading down that last column, the portfolio splits cleanly:

**Pattern A — the blocked switch.** A solution existed. Sometimes for decades.
The forces never resolved, so it was never hired. *(Wispr, Descript, Duolingo,
Khanmigo, Gamma, Granola)*

**Pattern B — no candidate.** No solution existed at any price. Pull was
literally zero, because there was nothing to be pulled toward. *(Google Lens,
Midjourney, Lovable)*

> **Pattern A is where the money is, and it's the one MBAs miss.** In a Pattern A
> market the job is visible, demand is visible, products exist — and everyone
> has already concluded *"people don't want this."* They were reading a blocked
> force as an absent job.

# PART 2 — What changed with LLMs

## 2.1 Three distinct drivers, not one

Not "AI got good." Three separate things moved, and they are genuinely
independent — each could in principle have happened without the others.

> **Driver 1 made voice possible. Driver 2 made voice urgent. Driver 3 is the
> bill that came due.**

---

### Driver 1 — The register shift: syntax → natural language

**What changed:** the *form* of instruction. The rudimentary layer of
human–machine interaction had always been syntactic — command lines, query
operators, form fields, menu paths, keyboard shortcuts, boolean logic. You
learned the machine's language. Now the machine takes yours.

**Why this is the enabling driver, and the one people skip:**

> **Before natural language, voice input wasn't merely inconvenient — it was
> incoherent.**

You cannot *say* `grep -rn "foo" .` You cannot say a menu path, a keyboard
shortcut, a URL, or `site:x.com filetype:pdf`. Most of what humans put into
machines for forty years **was not speech-shaped**, so there was nothing for
voice to transcribe even if the microphone had been perfect.

The register shift is what made speech a candidate modality at all. It is a
precondition, not an improvement.

---

### Driver 2 — The throughput shift: keywords → paragraphs, one-shot → trade

**What changed:** the *volume* of language a human directs at a machine, on two
axes at once.

**Axis one — utterance length.** Context helps. Constraints help. Examples help.
Saying what you *don't* want helps. For the first time in the history of
human–computer interaction, **the machine gives a better result for saying
more.** This reverses forty years:

| Era | Language the human had to produce |
| --- | --- |
| Command line | Terse, exact, memorised |
| GUI | **Less** — point instead of type |
| Search engines | **Less** — trained to strip queries to keywords |
| Forms and dropdowns | **Less** — prose replaced by fields |
| **LLMs** | **More. Much more.** |

**Axis two — the number of exchanges.** It isn't one input, it's a **trade**.
Prompt, read, correct, add context, retry. Each turn carries its own friction
cost, and the *marginal* turn is where that cost decides the outcome — because
turn four has uncertain payoff, high friction makes people stop early and accept
a worse answer.

**Why this is the urgency driver:** volume × cost-per-word = pain. And critically,
**the volume scales with how useful the models get.** Better models → more
prompting → more typing. The pain grows in direct proportion to the technology's
value. No prior interface generation had a push that fed on its own success.

---

### Driver 3 — The ambient shift: the room became a product constraint

**What changed:** typing is silent and private. Speaking is neither.

The moment Drivers 1 and 2 push the modality toward voice, **where you physically
are** starts determining whether you can work at all. An open-plan office, a
café, a shared flat, a train, a client site — none of these mattered when input
was silent.

**Why it's a distinct driver and not a footnote:** it has nothing to do with the
machine. It is a wholly new constraint *dimension* — social and acoustic —
introduced by the modality shift, and it gates adoption independently of how good
the software is. A product can win on Drivers 1 and 2 and still be unusable for
most of a user's working day.

This is the driver that produces **J12**, and it is the one Wispr has spent the
most engineering on: Whisper Mode, hold-to-talk, noise-robust modelling, and —
tellingly — published microphone guidance.

---

### How they compose

| Driver | Without it | What it produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Register** | Nothing to say — input isn't speech-shaped | Voice becomes *possible* |
| **2. Throughput** | Voice is a nice-to-have; typing three words is fine | Voice becomes *urgent* |
| **3. Ambient** | — | The constraint that decides *where* it's adoptable |

Driver 1 without Driver 2 gives you Siri: natural language, but such low volume
that nobody needed a better input method. **That's the control case, and it's
worth naming in class** — natural-language voice interfaces shipped to a billion
phones in 2011 and did not change how anyone works, because the throughput
wasn't there.

## 2.2 So the circumstance changed — and that is the correction

Go back to the job story. Earlier drafts of this analysis claimed the
circumstance was stable and only the *outcome* moved. **That is wrong**, and the
error matters.

| | Pre-LLM | Post-LLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Circumstance** | *When I have a thought and an empty text field* — arising a handful of times a day, for email and documents | *When I'm mid-conversation with a machine and need to hand it a paragraph of context* — **arising dozens of times a day, and a circumstance that did not exist in 2021** |
| **Who the text is aimed at** | **Other humans.** Bounded by how much I had to communicate. | **The machine itself.** Unbounded, and growing with how much I use it. |
| **What "more words" gets me** | Nothing. Wasted effort. | **A better result.** |

> **This is the real context shift: the arrival of machine-directed prose at
> volume.**
>
> Humans have never before had to produce large quantities of natural language
> aimed at a machine. That is a genuinely new circumstance, generative AI
> created it, and it is still growing.

## 2.3 Which means Push is new — not constant

Here is the corrected reading, and it inverts the earlier one:

> **Generative AI created the push before it removed the anxiety.**

The keyboard began hurting in a way it never had. Not because typing got slower,
but because the *volume* of typing exploded — and, critically, **the new volume
scales with how much value you get from AI.** The better the models get, the
more you prompt. The more you prompt, the more you type. **The pain grows in
direct proportion to the technology's usefulness.**

That is a self-reinforcing push, and no prior interface generation had one.

## 2.4 All four forces moved

| Force | Pre-LLM | Post-LLM | What moved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Push** | *Moderate* — typing bounded by human-directed communication | **Very strong, and structurally new** | **Machine-directed prose at volume.** Grows with AI usefulness. |
| **Pull** | *Moderate* — "3× faster", theoretical | **Very strong** | Not just faster — *finished*. And conversation **wants** to be spoken. |
| **Anxiety** | **Very strong** — training, enrollment, garbage output, looking foolish | **Collapsed** | The same technology that understands your instruction can clean up your speech |
| **Habit** | **Very strong** — 25 years of typing | **Weak in the new circumstance** | **There is no 25-year habit of typing prompts at an AI. That habit is two years old.** |

### The Habit row is the one people miss

The typing habit is only entrenched for the *old* circumstance — email,
documents, chat. In the **new** circumstance — prompting — there is no incumbent
muscle memory to displace, because the circumstance itself is two years old.

> **New circumstances have no incumbent habit. That makes them a land grab.**

It is very likely why voice-input adoption skews so heavily toward heavy AI
users: they are forming the habit fresh, and voice can take the slot before
typing claims it. *(Stated as a hypothesis — the public data doesn't break
Wispr's 60 billion words down by destination. Worth flagging to the class as a
claim to test rather than a measured fact.)*

## 2.5 This is what explains the timing

**The puzzle:** GPT-3 shipped in 2020. LLM-based transcript cleanup was
technically achievable by 2021. Wispr could have built Flow two years earlier
than they did. So why did mid-2024 work?

**An "anxiety collapsed" story cannot answer that** — the anxiety-collapsing
technology existed well before the product landed.

**The circumstance story can.** ChatGPT (Nov 2022) normalised sustained
prompting. Through 2023–24 machine-directed typing volume exploded across the
knowledge-work population. *Then* the keyboard started hurting in a new way.
Wispr pivoted mid-2024 and hit a push that had not existed when they founded the
company in 2021.

> **Wispr's real timing insight was not "LLMs can clean up speech."**
> **It was "LLMs have created a large and growing new volume of typing."**
>
> The first is a capability observation. The second is a market observation.
> Note which one is the product-thinking move — and note that they had spent
> two years building hardware for the version of the job that existed *before*
> LLMs created the new push.

## 2.6 The outcome changed too — and it split in two

The expected outcome didn't just rise. It **forked by destination**, and the same
utterance is now judged against two different bars:

| Destination | Outcome standard | Social job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A prompt to a model** | Intent must be captured. Grammar, register and polish are **irrelevant** — the model will cope. | **None.** Nobody sees it. |
| **An email, a Slack message, a document** | Must be sendable unedited. | **Strict** — nobody may be able to tell it was dictated. |

**This is why destination-awareness is the product and not a formatting nicety.**
Wispr is not adapting the *style* of output to the app you're in. It is choosing
between **two different definitions of success** from a single spoken input.

And it explains the adoption path: users start in the low-stakes destination —
prompting, where a mistake costs nothing — and graduate to the high-stakes one.
**The prompt box is the onboarding surface, whether or not anyone designed it
that way.**

## 2.7 The rest of the delta

**Products climb the job map.** Four of seven steps moved from user to product:

| Job step | Pre: who | Post: who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Define — decide what to say | User | **User — always, never moves** |
| Prepare — structure it into sentences | **User, in their head, while speaking** | **Product** |
| Confirm — check it's ready | User | User |
| Execute — produce the text | User, typing | Product |
| Monitor — spot errors | User, reading closely | User, reading lightly |
| Modify — fix what's wrong | **User, heavily** | **Product first, user lightly** |
| Conclude — format and place it | **User** | **Product, destination-aware** |

**You take share from non-consumption.** Not Dragon — *typing*, and *not writing
it down at all*.

**Outcomes shift from speed to trust.** Speed outcomes saturate: two seconds to
one second wins nothing. Trust outcomes don't.

**The job executor changes**, so re-derive the outcomes rather than inheriting
them.

**AI manufactures second-order jobs** — *verify what I didn't produce, choose
among forty, undo what it did, make sure it sounds like me.* Note where these
land: **every one is an anxiety-in-use job.** Citations, diffs, checkpoints,
"sounds like me" — the product craft of the next decade is anxiety management
for output the user didn't write.

## 2.8 The contrast slide

| | **Pre-LLM** | **Post-LLM** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who you type at** | Other humans | **The machine itself** |
| **Volume of machine-directed language** | Keywords and commands | **Paragraphs, and growing** |
| **Does saying more help?** | No | **Yes — verbosity is rewarded** |
| **Direction of interface evolution** | Type *less* each generation | **Type more — the reversal** |
| **Natural modality for the interaction** | Typing | **Speech — conversation is a spoken form** |
| **Push** | Moderate, stable | **Strong, new, and self-reinforcing** |
| **Habit in the new circumstance** | — | **None yet. Land grab.** |
| **The job** | Stable | **Still stable** |
| **The acceptable outcome** | Output I can work with | **Output I can send unedited — and forked by destination** |
| **What the product asks of the user** | Adapt to the machine | **Nothing. The machine adapts.** |

### The two sentences

> **LLMs made machines understand us, which made the interaction conversational,
> which made us produce far more language than we ever have — and conversation
> is a spoken form. The keyboard became the bottleneck in a job it had never
> been the bottleneck in before.**

> Generative AI **created the push and then removed the anxiety.** Most analyses
> only see the second, which is why they can't explain the timing.
